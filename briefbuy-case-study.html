01 Background

I had four weeks to build an AI product.
I shipped the design and the frontend.

In April 2026, I joined a four-week product challenge through an IT community. Our team of three set out to build a working AI shopping assistant for running shoes.

We chose running shoes because the purchase decision is surprisingly complex. Foot shape, running distance, budget, fit preferences, and injury history can all change what makes a shoe feel right. Even after hours of research, many shoppers still arrive at checkout unsure.

I led the product design and built the frontend, turning the concept into a usable prototype within the challenge timeline. It was my first time shipping production-facing code, and it changed how I think about the role a designer can play in bringing an idea to life.

02 The Problem

Chat is great at explaining.
It is bad at helping you decide.

By message ten, you can easily lose track. The longer a shoe conversation gets, the more your original conditions drift. AI has forgotten your wide feet and your budget.

A runner training for a first half marathon

Wide feet. Under $200. Nervous about blisters on long runs.

"I have wide feet and I am training for a half marathon. My budget is about $180. Is Hoka Clifton 10 or New Balance 1080 better for me?"
Both are strong options. The Clifton is lighter and soft. The 1080 has wide sizes and plush cushioning. For your feet and budget, I would start with the 1080.
"Can you keep the wide fit and blister issue in mind?"

04 Design

Chat on one side.
Decisions on the other.

The main design choice was a two panel layout. The chat is where users ask questions. The panel is where the decision stays organized.

The panel updates with every message, but it never moves. Users can always see their profile, candidates, fit scores, and rejected options.

Two panel layout

Left: Chat

Users paste links, ask follow ups, and narrow the list by fit, cushion, or price.

Right: Fixed Decision Panel

The profile, scores, rankings, and rejected shoes stay in one place while the chat changes.

Inside the design

Four screens. Each solving a different part of the decision.

The landing screen has one job: get the first product links pasted in. No sign up, no empty state to figure out, just a single input and a short explanation of what the app does.

Setting up a runner profile is optional here. Users can skip it and get a general comparison, or fill it in once and let foot width, mileage, budget, and pain points shape a more personal result.

briefbuy.app

The runner profile carries the details that change the recommendation. Users set it once, and the app keeps using it during the session.

Set once

The profile follows every comparison, so users do not have to repeat themselves.

Details that matter

Foot width, mileage, budget, and pain points all shape the recommendation.

Always visible

The profile stays at the top of the decision panel, so users can check it quickly.

The shoe detail view is where a user checks the reasoning behind a recommendation before committing to one option.

ScoreCircle breaks down the match

The ring shows the overall match score, and the bars underneath split it into biomechanical fit, specs alignment, use case, price and value, and reviews.

Strengths and considerations badges

Badges call out what the shoe is good at, like durability or energy return, and flag tradeoffs like a premium price, all at a glance.

Specs with a flag when it matters

Detailed specs like drop, weight, terrain, and stack height sit next to a small badge, such as Over Budget, whenever a number needs a second look.

The comparison lightbox opens up to three shoes side by side in full detail, so users do not have to hold specs in their head while scrolling between cards.

A crown badge marks whichever shoe wins the match score, and a "Best" tag calls out the winning value in each spec row, cushioning, weight, price, and more, so the differences that matter are easy to scan in one glance. Pros and cons badges sum up the tradeoffs underneath.

07 What I Learned

Building fast taught me
where the product was strongest.

The biggest lesson was simple: memory is part of the design. BriefBuy worked because the decision panel stayed visible while the chat kept changing.

I also learned where I would push the product next. I still think users should be able to describe what they need first, without finding product links before they start.
